# Sailing Knowledge and Physics

## Knowledge-Based Progression

Knowledge is the game’s most important progression system. The player should often realize:

> “I could have done this from the beginning. I simply did not understand how.”

Sailing is not unlocked by a conventional gate. The equipment, sea rules, gestures, and actions exist from the start; the player becomes stronger by understanding them. Discoveries include how to deploy and trim sails, identify wind, tack through the no-go zone, navigate with or without a compass, read stars and currents, recognize storms, reef sails, enter mystical waters, read tsunami signs, interpret symbols and false maps, navigate by sound, and exploit tides.

Instead of displaying “New ability unlocked,” the game should let notes, stories, environmental clues, observation, and experimentation reveal how systems work.

## Hidden but Available Controls

From the beginning, the player can deploy or reduce the sail, adjust its angle, stop the engine, anchor, inspect stars and the wind indicator, sail without fuel, use currents, and redistribute weight. New players may ignore these options because the engine is easier; experienced players can use them immediately.

This supports wind-only, no-engine, no-upgrade, no-chart, and minimum-loop speedruns.

> Hide knowledge, not functionality.

Avoid blinking highlights, floating interaction icons, and persistent prompts. Components still need clear physical affordances: ropes look pullable, wheels turnable, and sail latches interactive. Discovery must not require arbitrary touchscreen guesses.

## Sailing as the Core Gameplay

Sailing should be meaningful without becoming exhausting or inaccessible:

> Apparent physical depth, simple input, clear feedback.

The game can use authored forces, simplified vectors, animation, shaders, and tuned responses rather than an academically exact ocean simulation. The model may account for wind direction and strength, sail angle and area, hull shape, waves, currents, cargo weight and distribution, rudder angle, momentum, water intake, mast stress, sail damage, and hull damage.

Moment-to-moment decisions reduce to:

1. Boat direction
2. Sail area
3. Sail angle
4. Stability and weight distribution

Steering preserves momentum. A temporary wheel overlay may rotate continuously with haptic ticks, while the physical rudder is clamped to about 35 degrees per side. Centering the rudder stops adding turn force but does not cancel momentum.

Deeper conditions derive from those inputs: mast stress from wind and sail area; heel from wind angle, sail load, hull, and cargo; speed from wind, trim, current, and hull condition; turning from rudder and momentum; flooding from damage, impact, and heel.

Feedback should replace numerical dashboards. Use creaking wood, tightening ropes, sail flutter or snap, mast bend, water over the side, shifting cargo, character balance, wind sound, wake shape, sluggish handling, tiller resistance, flags and particles, foam bent by currents, hull wobble, and distinct haptic rhythms. Important forces should use at least two channels—visual, audio, animation, or haptic—where practical. Haptics enhance but never carry essential information.

Do not show “Mast stress: 78 percent”; make the strain perceptible.

## Sailing Knowledge Sequence

### Stage One: Deploying the Sail

The engine first loses power and burns fuel rapidly. It remains usable but unsustainable. A note, memory, environmental cue, or boat component suggests releasing the sail; incorrect trim may still leave the boat stationary.

### Stage Two: Reading the Wind

Wind is visible through mast ribbons, smoke, clouds, foam, flags, birds, rain, sail sound, and water ripples. The player learns that sail angle matters.

### Stage Three: Understanding the No-Go Zone

Trying to sail directly into the wind slows or stops the boat. Learning to tack in a zig-zag becomes a major mastery moment.

### Stage Four: Using a Compass

A compass gives direction, not location. It must be combined with silhouettes, coastlines, lighthouses, mountains, sun, chart notes, and travel time.

### Stage Five: Celestial Navigation

Stars, moon, and constellations become tools; some islands may only be found under particular night conditions.

### Stage Six: Reading Currents

Currents become oceanic equivalents of orbital slingshots. Some destinations require entering one at a particular angle.

### Stage Seven: Reefing and Storm Sailing

More sail is not always better. Smaller sail trades speed for safety and teaches strategic restraint.

### Stage Eight: Supernatural Navigation

Mystical waters can depend on silence, music, reflections, memory, tides, a missing star, whale calls, a compass pointing somewhere other than north, or sailing without directly viewing the destination.

## Sailing as a Puzzle

Travel should not be simple point-and-go movement. A nearby island may be inaccessible under current conditions unless the player accepts delay, damage, fuel use, current traps, or tsunami risk. Better solutions may involve another island, a different current, a weather change, or another approach.

Stationary islands form a dynamic route network shaped by wind, currents, tides, fog, storms, supernatural boundaries, and the tsunami cycle—the marine equivalent of orbital mechanics.

## Simulated Physics and Systemic Conditions

Conditions may include global wind, local gusts, currents, wave direction and intensity, storm position, fog, visibility, tides, and supernatural disturbances. Fully random rules would undermine learning, so use controlled systemic variation.

Geography and major laws remain stable while open-sea conditions vary within understandable expedition-specific bounds. Unfavorable routes should cost time, fuel, skill, or an intermediate stop, but rarely make a destination impossible.

Examples:

* the northern current persists but changes strength
* an island always rejects engines, while warning intensity varies
* tsunami signs always form a learnable sequence
* a storm follows a known corridor at varying speed
* a fog island always uses sound, while its exact safe passage changes

Players learn stable laws while adapting to conditions.
