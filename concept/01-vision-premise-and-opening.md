# Vision, Premise, and Opening

## High-Level Vision

The game is intended to be a spiritual love letter to *Outer Wilds*, not by copying its setting or mechanics directly, but by recreating its core emotional experience:

* curiosity-driven exploration
* knowledge-based progression
* a mysterious world governed by discoverable rules
* a repeating catastrophe
* environmental storytelling
* a growing realization that the original personal goal is only one part of a much larger mystery

Instead of outer space, planets, and orbital mechanics, the game is set across a mysterious ocean filled with islands.

The sea replaces space.

The islands replace planets.

Sailing conditions replace orbital movement.

A supernatural tsunami creates the repeating expedition rhythm, but unlike the supernova it does not kill the player or erase the run. It is a defensive force belonging to the mystical islands: the sea rejects foreign objects and sends the boat back toward the human world.

Atlantis, or an equivalent lost island, plays a role similar to the Eye of the Universe, but it is not presented as the player’s goal at the beginning of the game.

The central fantasy is not simply “exploring islands.” It is:

> Becoming a true sailor by learning how to understand a sea that modern technology cannot conquer.

The game should communicate that the sea is not merely an obstacle or enemy. It is a system, a language, and perhaps a living or supernatural force that the player must gradually learn to read.

---

## Core Premise

The player character grew up on a starting island and currently works as an ordinary fisherman.

Their parents disappeared when the player was still young.

The parents were archaeologists and explorers who had discovered evidence of a mysterious lost island or civilization. During their search for it, they vanished.

However, the game does not begin with the player actively searching for Atlantis.

Instead, it begins with a normal fishing trip.

The player is operating a small fishing boat powered by an engine and returning to port with the day’s catch.

This opening serves several purposes:

* it introduces movement and boat control naturally
* it establishes the player’s ordinary life
* it introduces the starting island and port community
* it gives the player a reason to already understand basic engine-powered navigation
* it creates a contrast between the safe, familiar sea and the supernatural waters encountered later
* it avoids beginning with a dramatic prophecy, chosen-one narrative, or immediate quest for Atlantis

The player initially appears to be an ordinary working person living in a coastal community.

Only after returning to port do rumors and memories about the player’s parents begin to emerge.

---

## The Opening Sequence

The first playable section is a fishing trip.

The player is already at sea and must bring the boat and its catch safely back to the harbor.

The early controls should be simple and intuitive.

The player learns:

* how to start or control the engine
* how to use the throttle
* how to steer
* how to read basic instruments
* how to avoid shallow water or rocks
* how to approach the harbor
* how to slow down
* how to dock
* possibly how to protect the catch from rough handling

This section is not presented as a traditional tutorial.

The player is simply completing the character’s normal daily work.

The player should feel comfortable and capable when using the engine. Engine-powered sailing belongs to the known and civilized world.

When the player reaches land, they can speak with people around the harbor.

The port community gradually reveals information about the missing parents.

Different residents may know different fragments:

* a fuel seller remembers that the parents purchased unusually large amounts of fuel
* a shipwright remembers that they requested a sailing mast on an engine-powered vessel
* an old fisherman claims that engines do not work in the waters where they disappeared
* a merchant remembers unusual supplies they purchased
* a former friend of the parents believes they were chasing a myth
* someone may suggest they abandoned the player
* someone else may insist they died in a storm
* another person may believe they reached a forbidden island

The information should be fragmented, contradictory, and personal.

The player’s first motivation is therefore simple:

> Find out what happened to their parents.

The mystery of Atlantis emerges only later.

## World Structure

The world contains approximately twenty islands.

These are divided into two broad categories.

### Twelve Ordinary Islands

The ordinary islands belong to the human world.

They can be reached relatively easily using the boat’s engine.

They may include:

* fishing settlements
* trading ports
* industrial or repair islands
* farming communities
* military ruins
* lighthouse islands
* archaeological sites
* abandoned villages
* ship graveyards
* religious communities
* islands with local myths
* islands connected to the parents’ past

These islands provide:

* social interaction
* supplies
* trade
* fishing
* crafting materials
* ship repairs
* rumors
* records
* maps
* local history
* evidence about the parents
* preparation for more dangerous voyages

Travel between these islands should be relatively fast and accessible.

This prevents every journey from becoming exhausting and allows the player to participate in the world’s economy and social life without always engaging with the most difficult sailing mechanics.

The ordinary sea represents the domain of human knowledge and technology.

Humans have built:

* charts
* harbors
* lighthouses
* trade routes
* weather observations
* fuel infrastructure
* navigation markers
* repair facilities

It is predictable enough to be managed with modern tools.

### Eight Mystical Islands

The remaining islands exist within supernatural or anomalous waters.

These islands are closer in function to the planets of *Outer Wilds*.

They should not simply be ordinary islands with stronger enemies or larger waves.

Each should be governed by a distinct environmental rule, navigational challenge, or supernatural law.

When the player approaches one of these islands, modern technology becomes less efficient or less trustworthy without becoming completely unusable.

The engine may lose power, consume more fuel, or respond unevenly.

Electronic equipment may become unreliable.

The compass may behave strangely.

Radio transmissions may distort.

Maps may become inaccurate.

The player can still brute-force some situations with the engine, but sailing, currents, stars, sound, observation, and accumulated knowledge are more efficient and reliable. The sea should reveal this difference clearly enough that players discover sailing as an advantage rather than experiencing it as a forced replacement for the engine.

The mystical sea represents a domain that cannot be conquered through technology alone.

---

## Transition from Engine to Sail

The engine should not stop instantly when the player crosses an invisible boundary.

That would make the system feel artificial and would force one correct playstyle.

Instead, the loss of engine efficiency should be gradual and readable. Sailing remains available from the beginning and becomes the elegant answer, while the engine remains a costly but sometimes useful tool.

Possible warning signs include:

1. The compass needle begins to vibrate.
2. Radio static contains fragments of voices.
3. The engine starts losing power.
4. Birds turn away or disappear.
5. The sea becomes unnaturally calm.
6. The air pressure seems to change.
7. Fog forms without explanation.
8. The engine sound becomes irregular.
9. Instruments stop agreeing with one another.
10. The engine labors, loses efficiency, and consumes fuel faster.
11. Wind begins to move through the sails even if the surrounding sea appears still.

A new player may believe the boat has malfunctioned.

An experienced player understands that they have entered a threshold region.

A skilled player may shut down the engine voluntarily, prepare the sails, secure cargo, and enter the region intentionally.

This creates an important distinction between mechanical progression and knowledge progression.

The world has not changed.

The player has learned how to read it.
